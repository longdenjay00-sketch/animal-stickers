# Golden Eagle

**Aquila chrysaetos** · IUCN Least Concern · CITES Appendix II

## Lore

The golden wash on the nape is why people named it royal. Kazakh and Mongol falconers still fly eagles at foxes and, in the Altai, at wolves. Rome put the eagle on the legionary standard; when a standard fell, the legion had lost its soul. Mexico, Albania, Germany, Austria, and Kazakhstan all take this bird (or its silhouette) as a national emblem. In North America many Indigenous nations treat eagle feathers as living medicine. The hieroglyph of the eagle in Egypt stood for the soul after death. The sticker’s hooked beak is the same tool that has stooped on hares since the Pleistocene.

## Facts

- The world’s most widespread eagle: mountains, steppe, and open country across the Holarctic.
- Length 66–102 cm; wingspan **180–230 cm**; mass 3–6.7 kg. Females are larger.
- Named for the gold feathers on the crown and hindneck. Body plumage is dark brown; adult plumage is complete at about five years.
- Hunts from soaring flight and from a stoop that can reach **150–200 mph**. Level flight around 80 mph.
- Prey is mostly medium mammals and birds (hares, rabbits, grouse); capable of taking ungulate young and, in trained birds, wolves.
- Six subspecies; *A. c. canadensis* is the North American form. Linnaeus described the species in 1758 as *Falco chrysaëtos*, type locality Sweden.
- Global population estimated **85,000–160,000** mature birds, trend stable. Wild lifespan 20–30 years.

## Known history

Pleistocene fossils are dense. Rancho La Brea in southern California yielded more than 690 golden eagle specimens — the most abundant bird in those pits. Middle Palaeolithic people in northeastern Italy and the Rhône valley used eagle talons. The species survived the end-Pleistocene megafauna crash and remains a cliff-nester wherever updrafts and prey still meet.

Persecution (shooting, trapping, poisoning) cut numbers across North America and Eurasia in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Legal protection, CITES Appendix II, and national red lists now cover most of the range. Threats that remain: electrocution on power lines, collision with vehicles and energy infrastructure, lead fragments in gut piles, and illegal trade in feathers and carcasses. Northern nesters in Alaska and Canada still migrate thousands of kilometres to wintering grounds.
